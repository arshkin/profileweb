A field-ready link to the vehicle.

The ground station is the team's only live window into the rocket during flight. It receives telemetry from both stages and has to be set up, powered and operated at a remote launch site.

The station combines a mechanical frame and enclosure, RF communication equipment, a computer and router, power distribution and thermal management. Version 2.0 is a ground-up rework of how all of those pieces are packaged, mounted, wired and accessed, aimed at reliability, maintainability and easier field use.

The program's longer-term goal is a vehicle capable of reaching the 100 km Kármán line, so the ground station needs to be a dependable piece of support equipment, not a one-off.

What needed to change.

The original station worked, but it had grown component by component. Improving it meant addressing several system-level issues at once:

  • Mounting. Electronics needed purpose-built internal supports rather than ad-hoc placement.
  • Wiring. Cable routing and harnessing needed to be organized so faults can be traced and fixed quickly in the field.
  • Access. Power hardware and connectors needed to be reachable without disassembling the station.
  • Heat. A closed box full of electronics needs a planned airflow path.
  • RF. Antennas need clearance from the structure and from each other.
  • Rigidity. The structure has to survive transport and repeated setup.

What I own vs. what the team does.

The system also carries two independent radio/antenna channels (one for the booster, one for the sustainer), which drives RF packaging and antenna placement.

Designing around the interfaces.

The redesign starts from the components and their connections, then builds the structure around them.

  • Aluminum T-slot frame for a rigid, reconfigurable structure that can be adjusted as the layout evolves.
  • Acrylic panels for enclosure walls with visibility into the internals.
  • 3D-printed mounts designed in Siemens NX to hold each component at a fixed location.
  • Planned harness routing separating power and signal runs where practical.
  • Ventilation and fans placed to move air across the computer and power hardware.
  • Antenna placement chosen for clearance from the frame and other hardware.
Trade-offs under consideration
  • Rigidity vs. mass. Stiffer structure helps survive transport but adds weight to carry into the field. A redesign direction being explored is using composite elements to reduce mass, with a target of 20%+ reduction.
  • Enclosed vs. open. Fully enclosed panels protect hardware but trap heat; vents and fans trade some protection for cooling.
  • Access vs. compactness. A tighter package is easier to carry, but leaves less room for hands and tools.
Mechanical–electrical interface checklist
  • Mount points match component envelopes and connector locations
  • Cable bend radius and service loops accounted for in the layout
  • Power distribution reachable from outside or with one panel removed
  • Antenna feed lines routed with clearance from power runs
  • Airflow path from intake to exhaust is not blocked by harnesses

Bringing the pieces together.

Integration covers the power distribution equipment, computer, router and RF hardware, plus the harnesses that tie them together. Work in this phase includes fabricating mounts, assembling the frame, and routing and securing wiring.

Bench first, field second.

Prototypes and sub-assemblies are checked on the bench (fit, wiring and power-up) before full integration. Telemetry electronics, operator interfaces and flight-data acquisition are validated together with the partner teams.

What this project is teaching me

Packaging is a systems problem. Every mount, cable run and vent hole affects something else, and the interfaces between disciplines are where most of the real work is.

  • Systems integration across mechanical, electrical and RF hardware
  • Leading a team and coordinating with partner sub-teams
  • Design for manufacturing, assembly and field maintenance
  • Making and documenting engineering trade-offs
