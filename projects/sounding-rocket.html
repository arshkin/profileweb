Electronics that have to survive a launch.

The Spaceshot program designs and flies two-stage high-power sounding rockets. I worked on the avionics structures side: the hardware that holds flight electronics in place and connects them to the rest of the vehicle.

The avionics carry flight computers, GPS and barometric sensors, batteries, telemetry radios and the pyrotechnic deployment interfaces used for recovery. All of it has to fit in a tube, stay secure through boost and staging, and still be accessible for arming and inspection on the pad.

Tight volume, high loads, short deadlines.

  • Packaging. Fit multiple flight computers, batteries and radios into a constrained avionics bay.
  • Mounting. Hold electronics securely against launch loads while keeping connectors reachable.
  • Interfaces. Route power, signal and pyro connections cleanly between boards and bulkheads.
  • Serviceability. Make the bay inspectable and repairable quickly when something goes wrong at the launch site.

Individual work vs. team accomplishment.

Structural

Assembly

Electronics have to be installed, wired and armed in a set order inside a tube.

Schedule

Launch windows don't move. Hardware has to be ready on the day.

Clear interfaces, inspectable hardware.

  • Defined mounting locations for each flight computer and battery inside the avionics enclosure.
  • Organized connectors so power, sensor and pyro lines can be traced and checked.
  • Accessible layout so arming, continuity checks and inspection can happen on the pad.
Why redundancy in flight computers matters

Carrying more than one flight computer means a single board failure doesn't cost the recovery event or the flight data. The structural consequence is more hardware to mount, more wiring to route, and more connectors that have to stay seated through flight, which is exactly the packaging problem avionics structures has to solve.

Inside the avionics bay.

From bench to airframe.

Integration means installing electronics on their mounts, wiring them to batteries, sensors and deployment hardware, and fitting the completed bay into the vehicle for both the booster and sustainer stages.

A three-day turnaround.

After a flight test, the team had roughly three days to troubleshoot, rebuild and prepare hardware for another launch attempt.

I took part in that effort: inspecting hardware, finding what needed fixing, and rebuilding avionics integration on a short deadline. It was the clearest lesson I've had in why hardware should be designed to be inspected and repaired quickly.

What I took away.

Lesson

Rocket development is a balance between structural reliability, electronic function, assembly constraints and launch deadlines.

  • Test early: problems found on the bench are cheap
  • Clear hardware interfaces make integration and debugging faster
  • Design for inspection and repair, because something will need fixing at the field
